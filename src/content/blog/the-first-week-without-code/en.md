Yesterday I was talking about how important it is to slow down at the start of a project.

## Greenfield or brownfield, same reflex

A greenfield project? Don't write a single line of code the first week.

A brownfield project? Same thing.

It can sound counter-intuitive, but that exact moment is when the biggest gains are made: in time, in energy, in money.

## Why it pays off so much

The earlier you are in a project, the more expensive bad directions get... and the more profitable good ones become.

And from experience: once you're knee-deep in the code, you tend to dive straight into the technical details, and lose sight of the essential thing, the why behind each choice.

## Slowing down is not sitting around doing nothing

It's the moment to take the time to:

- understand the project in depth
- choose / [challenge the architecture](/blog/architecture-decision-records/)
- decide what should be built, what shouldn't, and in what order
- understand [the business rules](/blog/the-value-is-in-the-business-rules/)
- [map out the business domain](/blog/model-orm-vs-domain-model/)
- look at what already exists and could be reused
- ... and so on

Each of these helps make the right choices, in the right order.

We're talking about 5, 10, 15 days "lost"... against months (easily) gained afterward.

## The cost of rushing

And yet, I see too many projects take the wrong direction.

Only yesterday, I put together a [quote](/blog/the-part-time-senior-developer/) for 10 days of business modelling... and the client (on a genuinely complex project) answered "no, too expensive, and we want things faster."

I hope for their sake that they stumble onto the right path by chance... but I already know there is a 9-in-10 chance the project will crash, end up costing twice the planned budget, or come out half broken and full of trouble.

## Set the keyboard aside

So on your next project, set the keyboard aside.

And once you think you're ready? Set it aside for a few more days. Really.

PS: we're talking here about projects meant to last. If you're still at the stage of throwaway MVPs to find traction, none of this matters. But when it's meant to last: go slow and do it well, so you can keep doing it well for a long time (and even faster and faster).
